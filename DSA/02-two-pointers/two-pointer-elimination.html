Two-Pointer Elimination
Pattern study · two pointers

Why two pointers work: every step deletes a whole row of pairs

Container With Most Water asks you to pick the best pair (i, j) out of n(n−1)/2 candidates. Brute force checks every cell of that pair grid. Two pointers checks one cell, proves a whole row or column can't win, and throws it away. Step through it and watch the right-hand grid get cleared.

The one-line proof

Why moving the shorter wall is safe

Say the left wall is the shorter one, h[L] ≤ h[R]. Keep L fixed and imagine every other partner j between the pointers:

area(L, j) = min(h[L], h[j]) × (j − L)
          ≤ h[L] × (j − L)      ← height can never exceed the short wall
          ≤ h[L] × (R − L)      ← and the width only shrinks
          = area(L, R)              ← which you just measured

So every pair left in row L is no better than a number you already hold. The short wall has nothing left to offer, and you drop it. Moving the taller wall would be a mistake: the new container is narrower and still capped by the same short wall, so you'd throw away pairs you haven't proven anything about.

That's the whole trick. Each step spends one comparison to discard up to R − L − 1 pairs, and after n − 1 steps the grid is empty. You've covered all n(n−1)/2 pairs while computing only n − 1 of them, so it runs in O(n) time and O(1) space.

Spotting the pattern

Three questions that point to two pointers

Is the answer a pair or a window?

The search space is (i, j) with i < j over a line of elements, which is the upper triangle you see above.

Does looking at the two ends settle one of them?

Sorted order, or a quantity that only moves one way (width always shrinks inward), lets one comparison rule out an end for good.

Can you start at a corner?

Starting at (0, n−1) means each comparison has exactly two moves, and one of them is provably safe. If neither move is provably safe, the pattern doesn't apply.

The family

Same grid, different reason for deleting a row

Most two-pointer problems follow the elimination logic above. A few just walk inward from both ends because the property is defined by mirrored positions. It helps to know which kind you're looking at.

The last row is the real test. Two pointers is not about two indices. It's about having a monotone structure that lets one comparison discard a whole row or column. Without that structure, like in unsorted Two Sum, you fall back to a hashmap. The same staircase walk over a grid shows up in Search a 2D Matrix II, where the grid is literal instead of implied.

The code

What the walkthrough runs

def maxArea(height: list[int]) -> int:
    L, R = 0, len(height) - 1
    best = 0
    while L < R:
        best = max(best, min(height[L], height[R]) * (R - L))
        if height[L] < height[R]:
            L += 1      # row L can't beat what we just measured
        else:
            R -= 1      # column R can't either (ties: either side is safe)
    return best
